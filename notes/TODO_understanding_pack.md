## What was asked, in Jonas's terms

Purpose: a way for Jonas **and others** to understand how the active-inference model works —
well enough to build on the concept (and possibly on the published Nature-paper code), both
toward **other scenarios** and, more importantly, toward **comfort-zone boundaries (CZB)**.
The immediate focus is understanding active inference and all its intricacies.

Any medium is allowed: slides, documents, figures/flowcharts, software overview
documentation — whatever makes it understandable and buildable-on.

The audience is **not very mathy**: they can read and understand most math, but the main
text must be formulated in other terms — components, inputs, outputs. The math goes in
**notes for those interested, at different levels of abstraction**.

### The specific questions (verbatim intent, lettered as given)

- **(a) Scenario switching.** What is different for the different scenarios, exactly? What
  are the decisions that need to be made/implemented when you switch scenario?
- **(b) Normal vs critical.** What is different if you consider normal driving vs critical
  event responses?
- **(c) Crash causation.** Is there some way to *today* include crash-causation mechanisms
  (e.g., glances off road, drowsiness)? If not, propose how it could be implemented — and
  **be clear about what is speculation/our idea** vs what is in the papers.
- **(d) Normative driving.** How is normative driving defined for the individual scenarios,
  in detail? How can it be changed? What is defining it?
- **(e) Other agents.** What defines what the other vehicles are doing — including the
  particle-filter setup (or clarify whether that is how it is done)?

### The three overarching goals

1. Understand **what active inference is** (as instantiated in this model line).
2. Understand **how it can be modified** in the current context — what we would need to do,
   and how we would be able to **validate** a modification.
3. Understand **how to expand it into CZB** and what would be needed.

## Constraints and conventions

- House style (jonas-academic-writing): US English, markdown as source of truth, PDFs
  generated by `docs/build_pdf.py`, no period at the end of a heading.
- Artifacts must be regenerable: figures from scripts, decks from scripts.
- Claims must be traceable: paper/SI-sourced vs code-sourced vs our speculation, marked.
- Math in layered form: Level 0 (none, components/input/output), Level 1 (concepts with
  light notation), Level 2 (the actual equations, with SI equation numbers).

## Parked idea (Jonas, 2026-08-22): an interactive parameter-play tool

Jonas wants, later, a tool where a user manipulates parameters or choices and sees the
result in real time. Feasibility thinking (Claude, same date), three tiers:

1. **Static comfort-zone explorer — cheap and genuinely real-time.** Sliders for speed,
   assumed worst-case lead braking, reaction-time budget, a_limit, desired speed; live
   boundary curves (the closed form is milliseconds anywhere) plus field heatmap slices.
   Could be a single self-contained HTML page (closed form re-implemented in JS) or a
   small local Python app wrapping `src/comfortzone`. Also natural: preference-term
   shapes with sliders (the ch. 07 figure, made live).
2. **Belief/norm sandbox.** The norm tournament of ch. 06 as an interactive fan: drag the
   other vehicle's lateral position, watch the swarm's fan open/close; toggle norms.
   The lateral-only reimplementation in `make_diagrams.py::norm_tournament` is the seed.
3. **Closed-loop what-if — not real-time on this machine.** The full model costs ~18 s
   CPU per simulated timestep, so live closed-loop sliders are out; the honest
   alternative is a browser over *precomputed* runs — the OSF deposit already contains
   baseline + 7 ablations × full grids, so "pseudo-live" sliders that snap to the
   nearest precomputed condition would work today for rear-end.

Recommendation when picked up: build 1 first (an afternoon, immediately useful in
seminars), then 2; treat 3 as a data browser over the deposit rather than a simulator.
